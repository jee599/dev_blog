A customer asked our docs assistant how to rotate an API key. Claude answered politely and with total confidence: "I couldn't find anything about key rotation in your documentation."

The answer was on page two of their docs. The chunk was in my database. The embedding was fine. Postgres just never handed it over.

This post is about pgvector HNSW filtering: one innocent `WHERE tenant_id = $1` that made my retrieval return zero rows for 52 of 400 eval queries, with no error, no warning, and nothing on any dashboard.

## TL;DR

- pgvector's HNSW index collects `hnsw.ef_search` candidates first (default 40), and Postgres applies your WHERE filter afterwards. If 2% of rows match the filter, you get about 0.8 rows back, not 10.
- Nothing errors. `LIMIT 10` just quietly returns fewer rows.
- In my multi-tenant RAG (1.2M chunks, 40 tenants), 147 of 400 queries got fewer than 10 chunks. 52 got zero.
- Raising `ef_search` to 400 cut the short queries to 19 but tripled p95 latency. pgvector 0.8's `hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order` cut them to 3 at almost no cost.
- The last 3 came from a 600-row tenant. For tenants that small, skip the vector index entirely: an exact scan took 4ms.

## The setup

A docs assistant for 40 customer workspaces. One `chunks` table, 1.2M rows, `embedding vector(1024)`, an HNSW index with `vector_cosine_ops`, and a plain btree on `tenant_id`. The biggest tenant had 410,000 chunks (34% of the table). The smallest had 600.

Retrieval was the query every pgvector tutorial shows you:

```sql
SELECT id, content
FROM chunks
WHERE tenant_id = $1
ORDER BY embedding <=> $2
LIMIT 10;
```

The top 10 went into Claude with the usual instruction: answer only from the provided context, and if the answer isn't there, say so.

That instruction is why this bug lived for weeks. Claude did exactly what I asked. Empty context in, honest refusal out. A grounded "I don't know" looks like correct behavior on every metric I was tracking.

## Why does pgvector HNSW filtering return fewer rows than LIMIT?

Because the filter runs after the index scan, not inside it. HNSW walks its graph and keeps a candidate list of size `hnsw.ef_search`, which defaults to 40. Those 40 nearest neighbors come from the entire table. Postgres then discards every candidate where `tenant_id` doesn't match, and whatever survives is your result set.

So the expected number of rows you get back is roughly `ef_search × p`, where `p` is the share of the table your filter matches:

| Tenant share of rows | Expected matches in 40 candidates |
|---|---|
| 34% (410k rows) | 13.6 |
| 10% | 4.0 |
| 2% | 0.8 |
| 0.05% (600 rows) | 0.02 |

My biggest tenant was fine. Everyone else was eating crumbs.

It was worse than the table suggests, because chunks aren't spread uniformly. Generic questions like "how do I reset my password" land in a region of the graph packed with the big tenant's auth docs. A small tenant asking that question was competing with hundreds of near-identical chunks from someone else's workspace, all of them filtered out after winning the race.

Here's the rule of thumb I wish I'd known on day one: **with default settings and `LIMIT 10`, any filter matching less than 25% of your table can come back short.** That's just 40 × 0.25 = 10.


## How I measured the damage

I had been evaluating answer quality, never retrieval count. So I built a retrieval-only eval: 400 queries, 10 per tenant, each paired with a gold chunk I knew existed. Then I added the one log line that should have been there from the start:

```python
rows = cur.execute(RETRIEVE_SQL, (tenant_id, qvec)).fetchall()
if len(rows) < k:
    log.warning("short_retrieval", tenant=tenant_id, got=len(rows), want=k)
```

The results with defaults:

- 253 of 400 queries returned the full 10 rows
- 147 returned fewer than 10
- 52 returned zero
- Every zero-row query came from a tenant holding under 3% of the table

For those 52 empty contexts, Claude said "not in your docs" 49 times. The other 3 times it ignored my instruction and answered from general knowledge, inventing a settings page that didn't exist. Honestly, I'm not sure which failure is worse. The refusals were wrong but at least looked like a safe system.

## Does raising hnsw.ef_search fix it?

Partly, and you pay for it in latency. A bigger candidate list means more survivors after filtering, but every query walks a bigger chunk of the graph, including queries from the big tenant that never needed help.

| `ef_search` | Short queries (of 400) | Zero-row queries | p95 latency |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 (default) | 147 | 52 | 18ms |
| 100 | 81 | 21 | 29ms |
| 400 | 19 | 4 | 61ms |
| 1000 (max) | 9 | 1 | 140ms |

Even at the maximum value pgvector allows, 9 queries still came back short. You can't buy your way out of a 0.05% filter with a bigger list.

There was also a dumber problem. My first "fix" was `SET hnsw.ef_search = 400` at the start of the request, and the numbers didn't move at all. We run PgBouncer in transaction mode, so the `SET` ran on one backend and the query ran on another. Session settings and transaction pooling don't mix. Use `SET LOCAL` inside the same transaction as the query, every time.

## What is a pgvector iterative index scan?

An iterative index scan, added in pgvector 0.8.0, keeps walking the HNSW graph when filtering leaves too few rows, instead of stopping after the first `ef_search` candidates. It continues until it has enough rows to satisfy the `LIMIT` or hits a cap set by `hnsw.max_scan_tuples` (default 20,000).

```sql
BEGIN;
SET LOCAL hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order;
WITH candidates AS MATERIALIZED (
  SELECT id, content, embedding <=> $2 AS distance
  FROM chunks
  WHERE tenant_id = $1
  ORDER BY distance
  LIMIT 10
)
SELECT * FROM candidates ORDER BY distance;
COMMIT;
```

`relaxed_order` means results can come back slightly out of distance order, which is why the materialized CTE re-sorts them. There's also `strict_order`, which keeps exact ordering and is slower. For a top-10 that gets stuffed into a prompt anyway, relaxed plus a re-sort is fine.

Results with ef_search back at 40 and iterative scan on:

- Short queries: 147 down to 3
- Zero-row queries: 52 down to 0
- p95 latency: 24ms, up from 18ms

The big tenant's queries didn't get slower at all, because they fill 10 rows on the first pass and never iterate. The cost lands only on the queries that needed the extra work. That's the shape you want from a fix.


## When should you skip the HNSW index entirely?

When the filter is so selective that an approximate index is pointless. All 3 remaining short queries came from my 600-row tenant. Do the math: scanning the 20,000-tuple cap at 0.05% selectivity yields about 10 matching rows on average, so roughly half the time you land just under the limit.

But computing 600 exact cosine distances is nothing. So small tenants now skip the vector index:

```python
EXACT_SCAN_MAX_ROWS = 5_000

def retrieve(conn, tenant, qvec, k=10):
    with conn.transaction():
        cur = conn.cursor()
        if tenant.chunk_count <= EXACT_SCAN_MAX_ROWS:
            # tenant_id btree via bitmap scan, then exact sort
            cur.execute("SET LOCAL enable_indexscan = off")
        else:
            cur.execute("SET LOCAL hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order")
        return cur.execute(RETRIEVE_SQL, (tenant.id, qvec, k)).fetchall()
```

Turning off `enable_indexscan` stops the planner from picking HNSW, but bitmap scans are a separate setting, so Postgres still uses the `tenant_id` btree to fetch that tenant's rows and sorts them exactly. I confirmed the plan with `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` before trusting it. Exact scan for the 600-row tenant: 4ms, 100% recall.

If you have a few huge tenants and many tiny ones, partitioning by tenant or partial HNSW indexes on the largest tenants are the heavier, cleaner versions of the same idea. My routing hack was 6 lines and solved the problem I actually had.

Final scoreboard on the same 400 queries:

| Config | Short | Zero-row | Gold chunk in top 10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defaults | 147 | 52 | 241 |
| Iterative scan + exact for small tenants | 0 | 0 | 368 |

127 more questions where the right chunk actually reached Claude. No model change, no prompt change, no reranker.

## What should you check in any pgvector RAG pipeline?

- **Log rows returned vs. rows requested.** If you only log answer quality, a retrieval shortfall looks like a careful model.
- **Compute your filter selectivity.** If `ef_search × share_of_rows < k`, that filter value will come back short.
- **Use `SET LOCAL`, not `SET`,** if anything sits between your app and Postgres.
- **Re-sort after `relaxed_order`.** It's one CTE.
- **Eval retrieval separately from generation.** A grounded refusal on an empty context is a retrieval bug dressed as correct behavior.

So why does pgvector HNSW filtering return fewer rows than your LIMIT? Because HNSW grabs only `hnsw.ef_search` nearest candidates (40 by default) from the whole table, and Postgres applies your WHERE clause afterwards, so a filter matching a small share of rows leaves almost nothing behind. Fix it by upgrading to pgvector 0.8+ and enabling `hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order` with `SET LOCAL`, re-sorting the results, and routing very selective filters to an exact scan. Then add a log line for `len(rows) < k`, because Postgres will never tell you on its own.
